# MI325X FP8 Training: Optimize the Step, Not Peaks

## Executive Summary

- **Not A Proven Optimum**: The published evidence does not establish an optimum for your exact model, large-M shapes, block-scaling contract, and FP32 Muon optimizer. Keep TE HipKittens as the baseline, but do not equate selecting it with completing optimization.
- **TE Is A Defensible Baseline**: ROCm Transformer Engine documents HipKittens blockwise FP8 on **gfx942 and gfx950**, supporting both **1Dx2D and 1Dx1D** scaling and FP32 output. That matches important requirements of your forward/dgrad/wgrad split, but is not a shape-specific performance guarantee. [6] -> Compare complete forward/backward projection paths, not library names.
- **CDNA3 Has Not Acquired Scaled MFMA**: AMD explicitly distinguishes CDNA4's block-scaled MFMA instructions from CDNA3, which lacks them. [4] -> Software pipelining can improve scale handling, but an updated ROCm cannot add this instruction capability to MI325X.
- **Keep Scaling Changes Separate**: Your **128-element** block recipe is not interchangeable with 32-element MXFP8, tensorwise scaling, or rowwise-only scaling. TE support for additional recipes is not evidence of equal convergence or higher throughput for this model. [6][2] -> Treat these as different-math experiments.
- **Producer Fusion Is Still Open**: Your RMSNorm/SwiGLU FP8 producers are disabled. Enabling them is a plausible remaining same-recipe optimization, provided they reproduce the current BF16 rounding boundary, scale calculation, and quantization. A measured end-to-end gain for this exact configuration is **not publicly confirmed**.
- **Large-K Does Not Automatically Favor Split-K**: Your gate/up wgrad already has thousands of output tiles under ordinary tilings. -> Benchmark split counts rather than assuming that a reduction length of **65,536** requires aggressive splitting.
- **Published Training Rates Are Context, Not A Ceiling**: AMD reports Llama 3.1 8B FP8 training at **15,105 tokens/s/GPU on an eight-MI325X server**, versus **12,072 on an eight-MI300X server**, both at sequence length **8,192**. These are different models and configurations, not measurements of your single-GPU recipe. [13] -> Do not scale them inversely by parameter count and call the result an upper bound.

**Bottom line:** further optimization remains credible, especially producer/quantization traffic, shape-specific backward GEMMs, and full-step scheduling. However, the available sources do **not** justify a numerical practical-throughput ceiling or a guaranteed speedup for your exact recipe.

## 1. ROCm Version And The Backend To Keep

### Version scope

The current release documentation retrieved for this review identifies **ROCm Core SDK 10.1.0**, dated **October 5, 2026**. It lists gfx942 support in its framework compatibility entries and MI325X in its virtualization support table. [8] This is the current Core SDK version in the available documentation, not a claim that every PyTorch/TE training container uses that version. Pin the actual container, PyTorch build, TE commit, and kernel submodules when comparing results.

The distinction matters because the HipKittens paper's evaluation uses a **ROCm 7.0 beta environment**, while AMD's later training-performance tables use specific Primus container releases. These are different software baselines. [7][13]

### Backend recommendation for your contract

| Candidate | What the evidence supports | Recommendation for this recipe |
|---|---|---|
| **TE HipKittens blockwise FP8** | Documented on gfx942/gfx950; 1Dx2D and 1Dx1D; BF16/FP32/FP16 output; K and N multiples of 16. [6] | **Keep as the reference implementation.** Its documented interface matches the required scaling families and FP32 wgrad output. |
| **AITER block-scaled GEMM plus local wgrad** | AMD documents 1x128 activation and 128x128 weight scaling in its DeepSeek inference integration, with model-shape-specific tuning. [14] | Retain as a challenger, but compare the complete three-GEMM training path, including quantization and transpose costs. |
| **hipBLASLt** | TE's general GEMM support table identifies hipBLASLt, while its separate blockwise section identifies HipKittens. [6] | Do not interpret the general hipBLASLt statement as proof that it implements your exact block-scaled path faster. Use it for supported baseline operations. |
| **CK** | Appears in AMD attention support and kernel benchmark baselines. [6][7] | A kernel-generation/backend option, not a demonstrated winner for these exact training shapes. |
| **Fully local Triton** | HipKittens reports advantages over Triton in its evaluated GEMM workloads, but not an exact-shape training comparison for your model. [7] | Benchmark it; do not assume either universal inferiority or superiority. |
| **BF16** | AMD publishes both BF16 and FP8 training results. [13] | Keep as a correctness and performance control, including for individual shapes where conversion overhead may erase FP8 benefits. |

**The recommendation is TE HipKittens as the incumbent, not TE HipKittens as a proven optimum.** No retrieved primary source reports the exact combination of M near 65,536, your N/K dimensions, FNUZ block quantization, independently quantized wgrad transposes, and full training-step timing.

The HipKittens paper is useful evidence that scheduling and data movement matter. Its evaluation includes MI325X and MI355X, and its benchmark protocol uses warmups followed by repeated kernel measurements. But the accessible results do not provide the requested exact-shape, block-scaled training table. [7] Its isolated GEMM results must not be relabeled as measured training throughput.

**Decision:** retain TE and run a per-shape, per-pass competition. AITER forward performance alone cannot decide the backend when your local 1Dx1D wgrad may determine the total.

## 2. Scaling: Preserve The Contract Before Changing The Recipe

### The hardware limitation still applies

AMD's architectural comparison states that **CDNA3 does not support block-scaled MFMA**; CDNA4 adds instructions such as `V_MFMA_SCALE_F32_16X16X128_F8F6F4`. [4] Its matrix-core programming documentation places the scaled-instruction example on **gfx950 with ROCm 7.0 or later**. [3]

There is an important correction to the recipe's wording: if scales vary along reduction K, scale application cannot be moved entirely outside the full reduction. Conceptually, the operation is:

`C = sum_b [scale_A(b) * scale_B(b) * dot(Aq_b, Bq_b)]`

The implementation may keep scale arithmetic outside the *inner unscaled MFMA accumulation for each block*, interleave it with other work, or pipeline it. It still must respect each K-block's scale. This distinction is an algebraic consequence of the supplied recipe, not evidence of a newly introduced instruction.

TE's HipKittens blockwise integration demonstrates that software support has advanced. The retrieved documentation does **not**, however, provide a measured gfx942 comparison proving that a newly fused scale-in-K-loop implementation is faster for your exact dimensions. [6]

### Which scaling alternatives are actually comparable?

| Scaling choice | Same numerical recipe? | Interpretation for MI325X |
|---|---|---|
| Current 1x128 activation/gradient and 128x128 weight scales | **Yes**, if implementation details match | Reasonable incumbent; matches the documented block-scaling families. [6] |
| Same blocks, better pipelining/layout/fused producers | **Potentially** | Preserve rounding, scale direction, scale values, saturation, and independent transpose quantization. |
| MXFP8 with 32-element scales | **No** | Different scale granularity/representation; do not assume CDNA4's native acceleration transfers to gfx942. [3] |
| Per-tensor FP8 | **No** | Changes which values share a scale; requires accuracy and throughput evaluation. |
| Rowwise-only scaling | **No** | Removing K-local scales changes quantization even when the FP8 element format is unchanged. |
| E5M2 gradients, stochastic rounding, FP4, low-precision optimizer state | **No** | Separate numerical experiments, outside the supplied baseline. |

AMD's Primus Flux guide illustrates why availability is not a convergence result for your model: it lists delayed, tensorwise, blockwise, and MXFP8 options, but does not establish their equivalence for a dense language model with your optimizer. [2]

Your insistence on independently quantizing the transposed wgrad operands is appropriate to the stated contract. Reusing transposed FP8 codes is not a free layout optimization when the quantization groups change. Likewise, keep `e4m3fnuz` and its supplied **240** maximum distinct from `e4m3fn` and **448**; substituting the latter changes the recipe rather than merely the backend.

**Decision:** keep the current block recipe as the controlled baseline. Neither the evidence nor the hardware supports declaring MXFP8 categorically better on CDNA3; nor does it prove the current recipe universally optimal for accuracy.

## 3. Fusions: Two Credible Same-Recipe Candidates, Two Math Changes

The decisive question is not whether an operation is called "fused." It is whether it eliminates materialization while preserving the values consumed by the existing quantizer and backward pass.

| Candidate | Status for your workload | Numerical classification | Recommended treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| **RMSNorm-to-FP8** | TE lists RMSNorm and cast support, but the retrieved material does not establish your exact blockwise fused producer's end-to-end gain. [6] | **Same-recipe only if** the existing BF16 rounding boundary, statistics, scales, and codes are preserved | High-priority experiment because your implementation already exists and is off. |
| **SwiGLU-to-FP8** | Exact FNUZ blockwise training measurement is **not publicly confirmed** in the retrieved sources | Same qualification; direct FP32-intermediate-to-FP8 output can differ from BF16-then-FP8 | Test forward outputs and backward behavior, not just GEMM inputs. |
| **FP8 attention** | TE documents AITER attention forward/backward on gfx942/gfx950, but this is not proof of FP8 support for the exact window/GQA combination. [6] | **Different-math**, because attention is currently BF16 | Do not count as an available same-recipe optimization. |
| **FP8 vocabulary head** | Exact tied-head streaming-CE implementation and measurements are **not publicly confirmed** | **Different-math** | Separate experiment requiring loss/convergence validation. |

### Producer fusion: preserve the intermediate contract

In your current execution, norm or SwiGLU produces a higher-precision tensor and a later operation quantizes it. Fusion can remove the write/read of that tensor and a separate launch. That is the mechanism behind the opportunity, not a measured speedup claim.

The subtle failure case is removing an existing BF16 rounding step. A fused kernel that computes a norm in FP32 and immediately quantizes to FP8 may produce different codes from a path that first rounds the normalized result to BF16. Classify that implementation as numerically changed unless it explicitly reproduces the old boundary. Also preserve any tensors required for backward; eliminating a forward write can simply move cost into recomputation.

### Attention: do not confuse BF16 backend coverage with FP8 coverage

ROCm TE documents AITER forward/backward attention kernels, while its broader support table describes attention as partially supported through multiple backends. [6] The Primus Flux FP8 guide leaves both attention FP8 flags **false by default**. [2] This does not prove FP8 attention is unavailable everywhere; it does mean the evidence is insufficient to certify your exact **window 512, GQA 18:6, head dimension 128, forward and backward** combination.

Your BF16 streaming head is another distinct boundary. Quantizing its logits-producing GEMM can change logits, softmax probabilities, and gradients even if the cross-entropy reduction stays FP32. Tied weights do not remove that numerical change.

**Decision:** prioritize the existing producer fusions under an explicit rounding contract. Keep FP8 attention and the FP8 head on a separate accuracy/performance branch.

## 4. Wgrad: Benchmark Split-K Against Existing Parallelism

For the supplied gate/up matrix, wgrad is conceptually:

`[11520, 65536] @ [65536, 2304] -> [11520, 2304]`

Its output contains **26,542,080 FP32 elements**, or **101.25 MiB**. These are calculations from your dimensions, not vendor benchmark results.

| Illustrative output tile | Output tiles before split-K | What it suggests |
|---|---:|---|
| 128x128 | 90 x 18 = **1,620** | Already substantial independent work |
| 256x128 | 45 x 18 = **810** | Still many tiles; inspect occupancy and register pressure |
| 256x256 | 45 x 9 = **405** | Less parallelism; split-K may be more useful, but is not automatically faster |

These are candidate search points, **not published optimal tiles**. The right choice depends on actual kernel resource use and scale-handling overhead.

For an implementation that materializes one full FP32 partial result per split, partial-output storage alone is approximately:

| Splits | Partial storage |
|---:|---:|
| 2 | **202.5 MiB** |
| 4 | **405 MiB** |
| 8 | **810 MiB** |

Actual implementations can use different workspace and reduction schemes. Extra splits lose when shorter K loops and added scheduling fail to offset partial writes, reduction reads, synchronization, and reduced data reuse. Split-K can also change floating-point reduction order: call it **same recipe, not necessarily bitwise identical**.

TE documents FP32 output for its blockwise GEMMs, but the retrieved public documentation does not establish an optimal split count or workspace size for this wgrad. [6] Your TE setting **0** means TE owns the decision, per the supplied configuration; do not equate it with the AITER/Triton split-count conventions.

**Decision:** compare TE automatic selection against your alternative's 1/2/4/8 sweep, timing quantization, transpose, workspace handling, GEMM, reduction, and FP32 gradient accumulation together. Do not introduce the already-rejected `HIPBLASLT_WORKSPACE_SIZE` switch.

## 5. What Training Measurements Actually Establish

### Published comparisons

| Source/workload | Hardware and configuration | Reported result | What it does and does not measure |
|---|---|---|---|
| AMD Primus performance table, Llama 3.1 8B FP8 | One eight-MI325X server; sequence 8,192; batch-size field 7; `rocm/primus:v26.8`, table release October 13, 2026 | **15,105 tokens/s/GPU** | Vendor-reported training throughput; not your model, one-GPU run, or FP32 Muon step. [13] |

| AMD Primus performance table, Llama 3.1 8B FP8 | One eight-MI300X server; sequence 8,192; batch-size field 5; `rocm/primus:v26.5` | **12,072 tokens/s/GPU** | Training reference point, not a single-GPU ceiling. [13] |

| Primus TorchTitan guide | Llama 3.1 8B launch examples | No measured throughput extracted | Commands are not benchmark logs. [9] |

AMD explicitly describes its performance tables as observed reference points, not the peak performance its hardware/software can deliver. [13] The retrieved table also does not specify an optimizer or establish that its timing includes precisely the full-step work you require. It therefore cannot certify an FP32 spectral-Muon-inclusive rate.

The earlier AMD training blog reports testing conditions and training performance in **TFLOP/s/GPU**, including different FP8 and BF16 batch sizes. It does not supply the requested raw step logs or optimizer-inclusive tokens/s for your model. [11] No qualifying AMAYA or Megatron exact-model full-step rate was established in the gathered evidence.

### A defensible bound requires measured component rates

Using your supplied projection dimensions, the four dense projection groups contain:

- Per layer: **53,968,896 weights**.
- Across 24 layers: **1,295,253,504 weights**.
- Tied embedding/head matrix: **150,994,944 weights**.
- Sum: **1,446,248,448**, before any additional small parameter groups.

Assuming one forward, one input-gradient, and one weight-gradient GEMM, with two FLOPs per multiply-add and no recomputation, this gives:

| Component | Calculated GEMM work per token |
|---|---:|
| Transformer projections | **7.7715 GFLOPs** |
| Vocabulary head | **0.9060 GFLOPs** |
| Combined | **8.6775 GFLOPs** |

Attention, norms, activation functions, quantization, streaming-CE reductions, optimizer work, and recomputation are additional. Thus even this useful work count is not a complete step model.

If `R8` and `R16` are **measured effective TFLOP/s for these exact projection and head paths**, an optimistic serialized-component estimate is:

`tokens/s <= 1000 / (7.7715/R8 + 0.9060/R16)`

This is a **conditional engineering bound**, not a measured rate or universal hardware ceiling. The effective rates must include the required layouts/scaling and must not be substituted with marketing peaks. The real full-step rate also needs measured attention, non-GEMM, optimizer, and scheduling time.

At global batch **2,097,152**, your single-GPU accumulation has **32 microbatches** at microbatch 16 or **16 microbatches** at microbatch 32. Consequently:

`tokens/s/GPU = 2097152 / measured_full_optimizer_step_seconds`

**Decision:** there is no publicly confirmed practical upper bound in tokens/s for this exact recipe. Microbatch 32 is an experiment, not a forecast: microbatch 16 already supplies a very large GEMM M, and the memory/recomputation trade-off may outweigh launch savings.

## 6. Ranked Optimizations Still Open

The ranking below is a workload-based priority order, **not measured percentage gains**. Profiling can reorder it.

| Rank | Open change | Classification | Why it can affect end-to-end throughput |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 1 | Enable and validate existing RMSNorm/SwiGLU FP8 producers; fuse compatible quantize/transpose work | **Same-math only under the existing rounding/scaling contract** | Removes avoidable intermediate traffic and launches without changing the chosen FP8 recipe. |
| 2 | Select kernels separately for forward, dgrad, and FP32-output wgrad; include split-K/tile/workspace sweeps | **Same recipe; reduction-order differences possible** | A single backend need not win every pass. Wgrad's geometry differs sharply from forward. |
| 3 | Reuse quantized weight layouts/scales while weights remain unchanged across gradient accumulation | **Same-math if lifetime and stochastic behavior are unchanged** | Can amortize repeated weight preparation; first determine whether TE already does this. Do not cache stale weights across optimizer updates. |
| 4 | Tune BF16 streaming vocabulary-head/CE chunking and avoid repeated layout conversions or allocation | **Same recipe if reduction/rounding contract is preserved** | The head remains substantial BF16 work and does not benefit from projection-only FP8 improvements. |
| 5 | Tune activation lifetime/recomputation and compare microbatch 16 with 32 | **Same training recipe, subject to RNG and accumulation-order qualifications** | Can reduce recomputation or overhead, but larger microbatches can increase memory pressure. |
| 6 | Optimize the existing FP32 Muon/AdamW implementation, scratch reuse, and launch scheduling | **Same-math only if precision, iterations, coefficients, and update order remain** | Full-step timing includes this work; projection speed alone is insufficient. |
| 7 | Test FP8 head and, only with verified exact kernel coverage, FP8 attention | **Different-math** | Expands low precision into currently BF16 computation; payoff and convergence are unconfirmed. |
| 8 | Test tensorwise/rowwise scaling, MXFP8, altered gradient formats, or reduced optimizer precision | **Different-math** | Potentially trades scale handling or arithmetic cost against quantization/optimization behavior. No supported gain for this exact model is established. |

The highest-value experiments remove work around an already fast projection path. They should be evaluated against complete optimizer steps, with the same global batch and loss behavior.

### Ideas to exclude from the optimization list

- **Native scaled MFMA on gfx942:** unavailable in the cited architectural comparison, not an overlooked software flag. [4]
- **Small-M decode split-K results as training evidence:** the supplied prior AMD result uses MI355X/gfx950 and decode-oriented shapes, not your large-M FP8 training path. [15]
- **A blanket "Triton is slower" claim:** unsupported for your exact shapes, despite HipKittens' wins in its evaluated workloads. [7]
- **Switching to TE as a new optimization:** already done in your recipe.
- **Transposing existing FP8 codes for wgrad:** violates the supplied independent-quantization contract.
- **HIP graphs, `torch.compile`, and the rejected environment-variable spellings:** excluded by your project constraints; they are not remaining recommendations.

## Synthesis

The backend, scaling, and fusion questions have different answers. **TE HipKittens is a supported incumbent**, **128-block scaling is a defensible numerical baseline**, and **producer fusion remains an open implementation opportunity**. None of those statements proves an end-to-end optimum. [6]

The principal tension is that finer-grained scaling improves control over quantization while imposing software scale handling on hardware without native block-scaled MFMA. CDNA4 can change that trade-off through instructions; a software update on CDNA3 can only improve its implementation. [4] Similarly, moving attention or the head into FP8 may reduce higher-precision work, but it changes the numerical experiment rather than finishing optimization of the present one.

Across **mechanism**, **scope**, and **evidence**, the candidates differ materially:

| Approach | Mechanism | Scope/trade-off | Evidence strength for this recipe |
|---|---|---|---|
| TE/AITER/local kernel selection | Better scheduling, tiling, and scale handling | Preserves the quantization recipe, with possible reduction-order changes | Supported interfaces; exact-shape winner unconfirmed |
| Producer/layout/cache optimization | Removes materialization and repeated preparation | Can preserve math if rounding and tensor lifetimes match | Strong engineering rationale; exact measured gain unconfirmed |
| FP8 attention/head or alternate scaling | Moves more work into different low-precision arithmetic | Requires new accuracy/convergence validation | Exact workload support and gain unconfirmed |

The practical recommendation is therefore **optimize the current recipe first, change the recipe second**. Establish the full-step baseline, test the disabled producers, compare each projection pass including preparation/reduction, and account for the BF16 head and FP32 optimizer. Publish the resulting tokens/s as a measurement of this model and configuration, not as a fraction of advertised peak TFLOP/s.
